01 / Featured case study · 2025

VetCare | SailPoint ISC Identity Lifecycle Proof of Concept

VetCare is a single self-directed IAM learning project originally documented in my Vepy workspace and its VetCare ISC Notion page. I used a fictional veterinary hospital to explore how SailPoint Identity Security Cloud could manage employee access as staff join, change departments, or leave. My documentation covers identity lifecycle scenarios, role mappings, sample JSON requests, and an ordered Postman API test plan.

Identity lifecycle scenarios
01 · Joiner — Luna Shepherd, Veterinary NurseA new WorkTail HR record leads to a proposed ISC identity, PetID SSO access, a VetEMR Surgery Nurse role, and auditable provisioning events.
02 · Mover — Toby Retriever, ER → SurgeryA department/job-code change prompts a PATCH request, removal of ER Tech access, granting Surgery Tech access, and policy review to avoid accumulated privileges.
03 · Leaver — Luna ShepherdA terminated lifecycle state triggers a proposed PetID SSO disable, VetEMR entitlement revocation, and SIEM deprovisioning event.
Skills and design decisions
  • ✓Joiner / Mover / Leaver (JML) workflows, RBAC, and least-privilege access.
  • ✓Separation-of-duties (SoD) checks and removing old access during role changes.
  • ✓JSON and REST/PATCH request examples with planned Postman test sequences.
  • ✓Audit logging concepts and a 15-minute leaver access-disable target (design goal, not measured performance).
Project status and evidence

My Vepy and VetCare ISC notes from August–September 2025 include diagrams, role-and-permission graphics, test-step descriptions, and illustrative JSON payloads. They reference a Postman collection named VetCare_ISC_Postman_Collection_v2.json, but the original collection file, execution results, and any live SailPoint tenant integration are still being recovered or verified. This is a documented proof of concept, not a production deployment.

Planned next improvements

Recover and reproduce the API test collection, capture verifiable request/response evidence, add access certifications and SoD exception handling, and explore Splunk audit dashboards.

02 / Hardware & IoT

Automating a sliding glass door

In May 2025, I set out to build a lower-cost automatic sliding glass door opener so I could let my dog outside when I was not home. Commercial solutions cost around $400–$600, so I explored a DIY design with a target budget of about $100–$150. I programmed an ESP32, developed familiarity with Arduino-style microcontroller programming, and successfully operated the motor with Bluetooth remote commands (open, close, and stop), as demonstrated in my video. I ultimately stopped the project before installing it: the existing sliding glass door is old and difficult to move, and I was concerned the small motor would not have enough torque to open it reliably. Replacing the door was not financially feasible. Wi-Fi/internet access and an optional RFID/NFC pet-collar trigger were ideas for later development, not completed features.

Work completed & design goals
  • ✓Successfully tested Bluetooth motor commands: O = open, C = close, and S = stop (May 2025).
  • ✓Selected an ESP32 DevKitC (ESP32-WROOM-32D) and ordered a BTS7960 motor driver; investigated a 12V motor with a belt-and-pulley drive.
  • ✓Planned Wi-Fi/internet control so the door could be operated when away from home.
  • ✓Considered future RFID/NFC collar-triggered pet access, along with open/close limits, obstruction sensing, and a manual release.
Project outcome

Working proof of concept: I programmed the ESP32 and remotely controlled the motor, including open, close, and stop. I paused development before connecting the motor to my older, hard-to-move sliding glass door because its resistance raised concerns about the motor's torque. Replacing the door was outside my budget. The opener was not installed or tested on the door; Wi-Fi/internet control and pet-collar activation remained planned features.
